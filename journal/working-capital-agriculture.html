Why working capital tells the real story of agricultural growth

Agriculture can grow on paper while using more cash than expected. The investor-relations challenge is to make the difference intelligible before it becomes an unpleasant surprise.

Cash conversion is not a footnote to the growth story. It is one of the clearest ways to understand the quality, timing and resilience of an agricultural operating model.

01 / Capital Discipline

Follow cash through the operating cycle

An agricultural enterprise may pay suppliers before products are delivered to a distribution network, before seasonal demand arrives, and long before a customer settles an invoice. At each stage, the business can be commercially active while cash remains committed. That timing gap is central to assessing funding needs.

The familiar cash-conversion-cycle framework brings three useful measures together: days of inventory outstanding, days of sales outstanding and days of payables outstanding. It is a way of framing a question rather than a verdict. Local contracting practices, procurement calendars and product characteristics all affect the answer.

02 / Capital Discipline

Why agriculture deserves its own lens

Seed and crop-nutrition products operate with planting cycles, storage constraints, regulatory requirements and different patterns of farmer demand. Commodity trade can add shipment, price, collateral and counterparty considerations. Field programs and agricultural technology may create another profile of adoption and implementation spending.

In IAG's publicly described integrated model—regulated distribution, agronomy programs and field intelligence—these activities sit alongside one another. That makes it useful to understand each economic engine before consolidating the narrative into a single group-level growth claim.

03 / Capital Discipline

The investor questions worth preparing for

A clear investor discussion should show how management monitors the movement from procurement to sale to collection. The goal is not to manufacture perfect ratios. It is to understand the underlying controls and to distinguish temporary investment in growth from a persistent structural constraint.

  • What changes the cash cycle across seasons, regions and product categories?
  • How are inventory obsolescence, product quality and receivables concentration managed?
  • What assumptions support credit terms, sourcing commitments and stock levels?
  • How do financing needs change when volumes grow faster than cash collections?
04 / Capital Discipline

Turn operating detail into financial clarity

Investor relations becomes more useful when it helps connect finance and operating teams around shared definitions. If a cash-flow forecast depends on faster collections, investors should understand the evidence behind that assumption. If inventory is strategically necessary, the explanation should include both its commercial role and financing cost.

The most persuasive narrative is not always the most optimistic one. It is one that can be revisited, reconciled with subsequent reporting and adjusted honestly when conditions change.

The central idea

Working-capital discipline is the bridge between agricultural activity and credible growth funding. Explain the cash cycle before relying on a revenue headline.
